Let Them Get Learning?

The Transnational Promise of Education for Work in Kenya, 1945–1980

Geneva Graduate Institute, Department of International History and Politics.

"The aim must be to produce at all levels of African society morally sound, economically valuable citizens…"African Education in Kenya (the Beecher Report), 1949

My doctoral research examines the promise of education for work in Kenya between 1945 and 1980, the expectation, urged by colonial, national and international actors alike, that schooling would lead the young into a rewarding livelihood. The promise was forged across borders, by the British Colonial Office, UNESCO and the International Labour Office alongside Kenyan ministries, schools and communities. It governed the young by managing aspiration, raising it to draw them into education, then dampening it as the salaried jobs it pictured ran thin, redirecting them towards practical, technical and non-formal routes. The research follows the promise through four routes and outcomes:

  • university education, for a minority routed abroad through scholarships and fellowships;
  • technical training, through the polytechnic and the vocational school;
  • informal work, in the jua kali economy that absorbed those the modern sector could not;
  • unemployment and the school leaver as a category of officials' making.

Whenever the promise fell short, from the Beecher Report of 1949 to the Gachathi Report of 1976, its makers answered with a repaired or an entirely new promise, often drawing in further international partners. They blamed the shortfall on the aspirations of the young or the design of schooling and only rarely on the wider economy. Those aspirations, though, had been encouraged by the promise itself, with salaried work in town held up as the natural reward for schooling. The same pattern underlies today's debates on youth unemployment, skills and alternative provision, and official assessments of the political risk of educated unemployment.

The central question

How the promise was made, governed and remembered

How was the promise of education for work made across borders between 1945 and 1980, how did it govern the young and their passage into work, and how is it remembered by those who received it and helped to make it?

Approach

Sources and method

The thesis works from archives and oral history. It draws on four collections: the National Archives at Kew, the UNESCO archives in Paris, the International Labour Office archives in Geneva and the Kenya National Archives in Nairobi. Alongside them, I interview people who helped shape the promise and people who grew up with it, at the University of Nairobi and the Kenya Polytechnic above all, analysing policy alongside the experiences of those governed by it. The promise is understood in this study as an apparatus of ideas, institutions and instruments, from examinations and certificates to plans and scholarships, that governed the young by raising aspiration and sorting them between routes into work.

The case

Four configurations of the promise

Four configurations of the promise follow Kenya's shifting ties to the wider world, each organised around a figure of the young and a route into work.

The productive citizen

The late-colonial state, the Beecher Report of 1949 and, in the Mau Mau Emergency, the re-education of the disloyal through screening and the camps.

A national promise

Africanisation and the self-help of harambee, as examinations and certificates became the instruments of advancement for the aspiring citizen.

Managed expectations

Manpower planning, the unemployed school leaver and the turn to technical and non-formal routes. 'Let them get earning?'

Lost to engineering?

Graduates and trainees, the scholarships and airlifts abroad, and emigration as the promise's safety valve.

Relevance

Relevance for policy today

The promise of education for work still drives international policy. The 2030 Agenda commits states to equip young people with skills for employment and decent work (SDG target 4.4) and set a 2020 deadline to cut the share not in employment, education or training (target 8.6), which passed with the share barely changed. Youth unemployment and the share not in employment, education or training stay stubbornly high across much of the world, and in Kenya, Morocco and Bangladesh, among recent examples, young people have taken to the streets over the gap between their schooling and their prospects. Frustration finds other outlets too, from informal work to migration, with consequences well beyond the countries concerned. Beyond the structural causes lies a two-part political question: what is education construed as able to deliver, and what are the consequences when it seemingly cannot? That history is the subject of my research and the background to my policy work.
