Let Them Get Learning?

Education and the Transnational Governing of the Young in Kenya, 1945–1980

Geneva Graduate Institute, Department of International History and Politics.

"The aim must be to produce at all levels of African society morally sound, economically valuable citizens…"African Education in Kenya (the Beecher Report), 1949

My doctoral research examines the promise of education in the global South at the end of empire and after independence. It looks at the instruments of educational government — enrolment targets, examinations and certificates, manpower forecasts, scholarships and development plans — and the national and international actors behind them. Kenya serves as a textured, context-specific case of patterns found across the postcolonial world:

  • the expansion of schooling as a promise of advancement;
  • the narrowing of opportunity through selection and certification;
  • the 1970s rise of non-formal education as a "new orthodoxy", often a means of containing demand for formal schooling rather than meeting it.

The research gives a historical basis for current debates on out-of-school young people and flexible pathways. It supports an account of both the need for alternative provision and its persistent second-class status.

The central question

A question of government

How were the young of Nairobi governed through education between 1945 and 1980 by the futures it promised them, and how were the instruments of that governing constituted across borders?

Framework

Research framework

The research treats the promise of education as a dispositif, in Michel Foucault's sense: a heterogeneous ensemble of ideas, institutions, policies, plans and practices around a single conviction — education as the route to development and a better future. It centres on the formal education system, primarily schooling, and on the instruments of educational government: examinations, registration papers, certificates, scholarship awards, enrolment targets and manpower forecasts. Each instrument, applied to a young person, set an outcome and left a record. The promise worked through two mechanisms — aspiration, drawing the young into enrolment, study and examination in hope of advancement, and distribution, allocating them to places through permission and denial of movement. Education governed by selection and certification when the promise held, and by blame of the young themselves when it broke.

The case

Four configurations of the promise

Four configurations of the promise follow Kenya's shifting ties to the wider world, each organised around a figure of the young.

The moral and productive citizen

Late-colonial schooling under the Beecher Report of 1949, the registration regime over young Africans in Nairobi, and the re-education of the detention camps during the Mau Mau Emergency.

The aspiring citizen

Africanisation and independence, the Addis Ababa targets and the Ominde reforms, and the self-help of harambee where the state fell short.

The unemployed school leaver

Manpower planning, the naming of the "informal sector" in 1972, and the school-leaver crisis of the Gachathi Report. 'Let them get earning?'

The graduate and trainee

Higher and technical education, the scholarships and fellowships routing the young abroad, and emigration as the promise's safety valve.

Relevance

Relevance for policy today

The belief that education delivers opportunity still drives policy across the global South, now for the largest generation of young people in history. Schooling and higher education have outgrown the labour markets meant to absorb their leavers, and the frustration of educated young people has become a political force — from the protests across Kenya since 2024 to comparable movements in Nigeria, Uganda, Ghana and beyond. The same history bears directly on current debates over out-of-school young people and flexible pathways: it sets out both the case for alternative provision and the reasons for its persistent second-class status.
