Something for the People

Dr Julie Reddy on South Africa's National Qualifications Framework — the promise, written into law within a year of democracy, that lifelong learning would count, and the long contestation over making it real.

In conversation with Alex Howells. Recorded over Zoom.

Dr Julie Reddy

A South African education specialist who served as chief executive of the South African Qualifications Authority (SAQA) — the body that built and runs the country's National Qualifications Framework — and spent much of her career inside the machinery of that framework.

In 1995, a year after South Africa's first democratic election, the new parliament passed its first piece of transformative legislation. It was not about land, or policing, or the economy. It was the SAQA Act, which created the South African Qualifications Authority and, with it, a National Qualifications Framework intended to record and rank every qualification in the country. “It showed how important education was,” says Julie Reddy, who would later run SAQA as its chief executive. “The first piece of transformative legislation passed by the new parliament. I mean, big deal.”

The promise behind it was that, in the new South Africa, lifelong learning would count — that what a person had learned would be recognised, whoever they were and however they had come by it. After a century and a half in which education had been rationed by race, that was no small undertaking. South Africa was also among the first countries anywhere to attempt it, one of a small group of “first-generation” frameworks alongside New Zealand, Australia and Scotland, and Reddy spent much of her career inside the machinery that resulted.

IA system built to exclude

To understand what the framework was for, Reddy starts with what it replaced. Apartheid education was, in her plain description, “racially segregated and unequal”, laid over a colonial legacy in which whole communities had been kept from schooling altogether. “People were deliberately restricted and kept from education and learning,” she says of those classified Indian, African or coloured. Where the state provided schooling for them at all, it did so “on a limited basis”, with resources, facilities and curriculum rationed by race, so that even the quality of what was on offer was, in her word, “compromised”.

The administrative machinery matched the ideology. The country ran, she recalls, “nineteen separate education departments” — national, provincial and municipal, divided by race — an arrangement she calls “pretty crazy”. And it had barriers built into it that had nothing to do with ability. Afrikaans, seen by many as the main instrument of the state, was a compulsory school-leaving subject and a site of protest. “If you did not pass the subject of Afrikaans… then you couldn't enter university. You could have had straight A's in every other subject.” The Soweto uprising of 1976 grew directly out of that imposition. Vocational and workplace learning, meanwhile, went largely unrecognised, and many Black South Africans were legally barred from certifying as tradespeople or apprentices at all.

IIThe first act

When the Mandela government took office in 1994, education was where it chose to move first. “The first thing they wanted to address is the issue of education for all — equal access, redress, opportunity creation, integration,” Reddy says, the constitutional principles of the new state. The SAQA Act followed the next year.

Why education, and why so fast? Partly, she suggests, because it was something the government could deliver, and deliver visibly. “For a hundred and fifty years people were denied easy access, equal access.” Recognising learning was “something they could give back to the people”, a “stabilising” act that showed the state was serving citizens who, in most cases, had never held a post-school qualification and often had not finished school. It was also a matter of capacity, since the new country needed “people who had the knowledge and the training to start this new country and put it on a different trajectory”. And it was about opportunity for the many South Africans who, she says, “were very talented, could do the work, would have earned the qualification had they had the opportunity”.

Beneath all of it ran a promise of transparency in a system that had been rigged for generations. Once a qualification is registered on the framework, Reddy notes, anyone can look it up — its learning outcomes, its level descriptors, how it is assessed — and, under the constitution, challenge an institution they believe has judged them unfairly, in court if necessary. “It's a big part of human rights, social justice, all of those issues.”

IIIBuilding the framework

South Africa was not working alone, nor from a blank page. Its NQF was one of a handful of “first-generation” frameworks, alongside New Zealand, which had led the way in 1991, Australia in 1995, and Scotland and the UK soon after. Those countries “informed our thinking and provided expertise”. But the South African version was meant to do more than catalogue courses. Reddy calls it a “transformative NQF”, built to knit a fractured system together, “to bring education and training under one umbrella” and to find ways of recognising the vocational, workplace and experiential learning the old order had ignored.

On paper an NQF is just a register of a country's qualifications; in practice, she says, it is “a relational construct”, and South Africa built its version by statute and then a long suite of policies governing how qualifications are registered and how prior learning is recognised. The first framework had eight levels, from schooling to the doctorate, and its flaws showed quickly. Adult basic education was tucked into sub-levels beneath level one, and “once it's on a dotted line or a sub-level, it gets forgotten”, pushed to the periphery. Twelve years of schooling were compressed into four levels, while master's and doctoral study were “contracted” into two. Recognition of prior learning, “a very contested space”, at first counted only towards a whole qualification, not access or credit. Even so, the country was, she believes, among the first in the world to build a national register of individual achievements, with roots in record-keeping that reached back to the 1800s.

The design kept changing under the weight of its own contradictions. After years of review and “major contestation” — at one point, she says, “they wanted to kick it out” — the NQF Act of 2008 replaced the original law, expanding the framework to ten levels and dividing it into three sub-frameworks, for general and further, higher, and occupational education, each with its own quality council. Workplace skills bodies, the Sector Education and Training Authorities, had been set up in 2000 and financed by a levy on employers. The framework ended up housed in a new Department of Higher Education and Training, straddling basic education and labour. By now such frameworks are common — around a hundred and forty-seven countries have one, Reddy says, with the United States and Canada among the last holdouts.

IVTaxonomy and life

What Reddy conveys most sharply is the distance between the framework on paper and the framework in use. Some of the gap was haste and money. “We might have rushed it a little bit,” she concedes; the impetus was to move “quick and fast”, because the government “owed the nation the promise of change”, and the resources were not there. Above all, she learned, context is decisive. “An NQF that works in New Zealand will never work in South Africa,” with its particular legacies and constraints.

The framework's finest words were the hardest to honour. Comparability, parity of esteem, credit accumulation and transfer “sound really good in the taxonomy, but once you try to implement, they don't work”. A student who should be able to move between universities carrying credit runs into a funding rule that withholds money unless half the degree is done at one institution, and so repeats the work. Recognition of prior learning, now meant to grant both access and credit, founders because institutions “don't know how to give credit” for learning they did not assess, and because a qualification earned that way still carries a stigma — “they think you came in through the back door” — despite more than a hundred thousand such awards.

Other friction was structural. SAQA was meant to be the authority, but the three quality councils were established as autonomous bodies that, Reddy says, “don't see SAQA as the authority”, so much of her time went on “putting out fires” rather than the work itself. And the framework was widely misunderstood, even by those who lived off it. She cites a recent continental survey in which, on one reading, only one per cent of educational institutions and two per cent of employers actually understood their NQF — a finding she calls “jarring”. Universities turned students away for lacking a school-leaving certificate they no longer needed; higher education, dominant and protective of its autonomy, refused to register the “part qualifications” that would let a student who left after two years walk away with something rather than nothing. Even language was contested: an occupational qualification pitched at the level of an honours degree was denied parity because it was called a “certificate”, which drove SAQA to publish an “NQF-pedia” setting out, among much else, that in South Africa “a credit is ten notional hours”.

The same misunderstanding surfaced in one of the framework's more delicate corners. Asked, during her tenure, to verify the qualifications of public servants, SAQA ran into a problem that says more about the system than the people caught in it. Verification, Reddy argues, should rest on a person's highest qualification — the bachelor's or master's degree they actually hold — rather than on every rung beneath it. Instead, officials were routinely required to produce a school-leaving certificate, a far lower level on the framework. For some that was impossible to meet honestly: they had earned their degrees despite being blocked at school under apartheid, often because they had refused, as a matter of protest, to study Afrikaans. Confronted with a demand for a certificate the old order had denied them, a few altered those decades-old school records. For Reddy the episode is above all a lesson in how little the logic of the framework is understood — that each qualification marks a level of knowledge, competence and complexity, and that it is a person's highest achievement, not the rungs below it, that should be validated for employment. She argued for understanding over blanket punishment. “You cannot throw the book at people. You've got to understand the circumstances.”

None of this is finished, which may be the point. Reddy describes the NQF as “a living framework”, not “cast in stone”, and when asked how you build such a thing she reaches for other people's metaphors. A Scottish colleague, she recalls, compared it to the baseball film Field of Dreams — “if you build it, they will come” — because there was no script to follow. SAQA's first chief executive, Sam Isaacs, borrowed a line she renders as a Native American proverb, “you build a path by walking it”. Three decades on, South Africa has walked a long way. Its framework is real, copied across the continent and much of the world, and the promise that learning would count is, at least, written into law. Whether it counts in practice — whether the diploma is honoured, the prior learning credited, the certificate granted its parity — is the argument Reddy is still having, and the one the next reformers will inherit.
